# THE HAMPTERS OF ULYSSES

### Being an Account of the Great War as Told by the Soldiers of E Company, 506th Regiment, 101st Airborne Division, from the Beaches of Normandy to the Eagle's Nest

---

## TELEMACHUS

### I.

Stately, plump Sergeant Buck Mulligan came from the stairhead, bearing a bowl of lather on which a mirror and a razor lay crossed. A yellow dressinggown, ungirdled, was sustained gently behind him by the mild morning air. He held the bowl aloft and intoned:

— Introibo ad altare Dei.

Halted, he peered down the dark winding stairs and called out coarsely:

— Come up, Kinch! Come up, you fearful Jesuit!

Solemnly a young man came up the staircase. He was a slim, pale creature, with wire-rimmed spectacles and a face that had not yet decided whether to be handsome or merely interesting. His name was Stephen Dedalus, though the men called him "Dedalus" when they were feeling classical and "Stevie" when they were feeling drunk, which was most of the time.

— The sergeant wants you, said Stephen.

— He can wait, said Buck. The war can wait. Everything can wait except breakfast.

They ate. The bread was stale and the coffee was weak and the eggs were powdered and the bacon was a memory of bacon, a Platonic ideal of bacon that had never existed in any farmyard. Stephen ate without tasting. He was thinking of his mother, who had died in a Dublin hospital while he was learning to jump from airplanes, and of the letter he had not written, and of the sin he had not committed but felt guilty about anyway.

— You look like death, said Buck.

— I feel like death.

— Death is just a door. A door that opens onto a field in France where the Germans are waiting with machine guns and a very poor sense of humor.

Stephen said nothing. He was thinking of the word "door." Door. D'oar. D'or. The golden door. The door of the golden west. The west was where the sun set and where the war was and where he would be tomorrow, jumping into darkness with a parachute and a prayer and a rifle that he barely knew how to use.

— Are you ready? asked Buck.

— No.

— Good. The ones who say yes are the ones who die first.

---

### II.

The company assembled in the grey English dawn. They were a hundred and forty men, more or less, depending on who was sick and who was wounded and who had gone AWOL to visit a girl in London and who had simply ceased to exist in the way that soldiers sometimes cease to exist, not with a bang but with a transfer to another unit and a new name and a story that no one would believe.

Captain Sobel stood before them. He was a small man with a large voice and a face like a clenched fist. He had never jumped in combat. He had never heard a bullet pass his head. He had never killed a man. But he could do push-ups, and in the army, this was considered leadership.

— Men, he said, and the word hung in the air like a bad smell. Men. Menn. Mened. The Welsh word for a horse. The Greek word for a month. The Sanskrit word for a thought. Men. We are all men here, thought Stephen. Some of us are even human.

— Tomorrow we jump, said Captain Sobel.

Tomorrow. The word was a stone in Stephen's stomach. Tomorrow. To-morrow. The day after today. The day that never comes except when it does, and then it is today, and today is the day you die or don't die, and the difference between the two is a matter of inches and luck and whether the German private in the foxhole has good eyesight and steady hands and a mother who taught him to clean his rifle every Sunday after church.

— We are the best, said Captain Sobel.

The best. The superlative of good. The peak of the mountain. The top of the heap. The cream of the crop. The flower of American manhood. The boys who would end the war and save the world and come home to parades and girls and jobs that didn't involve jumping out of airplanes into the teeth of the Wehrmacht.

Stephen looked at the men around him. He saw:

Private Blithe, who was nineteen and had never been to bed with a girl and was terrified of everything, especially the dark, especially the silence, especially the noise.

Private Malarkey, who was Irish like Stephen and drank like a fish and fought like a demon and would die in a place called Foy, but he didn't know that yet, and the not-knowing was a kind of grace.

Sergeant Guarnere, who had lost a foot but refused to go home because home was New Jersey and New Jersey was boring and at least in Europe he had a reason to be angry.

Lieutenant Winters, who was quiet and competent and brave in the way that a stone is brave, by simply being there, solid and unyielding, while the world broke against him.

And Private Compton, who was young and handsome and would survive the war and become a teacher and never speak of it, and the not-speaking would be its own kind of wound, a wound that healed on the outside and festered within.

— Are there any questions? asked Captain Sobel.

Stephen raised his hand.

— Yes, Dedalus?

— What if the chute doesn't open?

— Then you die.

— What if it does open and I land in the wrong place?

— Then you die.

— What if I land in the right place and the Germans are there?

— Then you die.

— So the plan is to die?

— The plan is to kill Germans before they kill you. The dying is optional.

Stephen lowered his hand. He was thinking of the word "optional." Optional. Option. The act of choosing. He had chosen to enlist. He had chosen to jump. He had chosen to be here, in this field, in this dawn, in this war. And now he would choose, or the choice would be made for him, and either way, the result would be the same: he would live or he would die, and the universe would not care which.

## NESTOR

### III.

The history teacher was a small man with a large mustache and a voice like a rusty hinge. He taught the boys about the war, or rather, about the causes of the war, which was not the same thing at all.

— The Great War, he said, and the words echoed in the empty classroom like a prophecy of more wars to come. The Great War. The war to end all wars. The war that ended nothing and began everything and left a generation of young men dead in the mud of France and a generation of old men alive in the halls of government, which was perhaps the greater tragedy.

Stephen sat in the back row, drawing pictures in his notebook. He drew a hampter. A small, round-bodied rodent with moss-green fur and twitchy whiskers and tiny black eyes that held the weight of the world. The hampter was sitting on a pea pod, looking at the horizon, and the horizon was on fire.

— Dedalus! The teacher's voice cracked like a whip. What were the causes of the Great War?

Stephen looked up. He looked at the teacher. He looked at the blackboard, where the words "MILITARISM ALLIMANCE IMPERIALISM NATIONALISM" were written in chalk, and the chalk was white like bone and the words were dead like the men they had killed.

— The causes, said Stephen, were the same as the causes of all wars. Men who wanted what other men had. Men who believed they were right. Men who were afraid.

— That is not in the textbook.

— The textbook is wrong.

The class laughed. The teacher did not. The teacher was a man who had never been wrong, or if he had been wrong, he had never admitted it, which amounted to the same thing.

— See me after class, said the teacher.

Stephen saw him after class. The teacher's name was Mr. Deasy, and he was a Protestant, which meant he believed that Catholics were going to hell and that the British Empire was a force for good and that the Great War had been a noble endeavor, like a particularly violent cricket match.

— You are clever, Dedalus, said Mr. Deasy.

— I am not clever, said Stephen. I am just awake.

— Cleverness is a gift. Wakefulness is a curse.

— Then I am cursed.

— We are all cursed, said Mr. Deasy. The Irish especially. We are cursed with history and humor and a talent for losing. We have been losing for eight hundred years. We will be losing for eight hundred more.

— Then why do we keep fighting?

— Because the alternative is surrender, and surrender is death, and death is the end, and the end is nothing, and nothing is unbearable.

Stephen left the classroom. He walked across the parade ground. The sun was setting, and the sunsets in England were like the sunsets everywhere: beautiful and indifferent and completely unconcerned with the affairs of men.

He thought of the hampter in his notebook. The small rodent on the pea pod, looking at the burning horizon. The hampter did not know about the causes of the Great War. The hampter did not know about militarism or alliances or imperialism or nationalism. The hampter knew only that the forest was in danger and that someone had to do something and that someone was him, even though he was three inches tall and the danger was enormous and the something he could do was very small.

Stephen envied the hampter.

---

## PROTEUS

### IV.

Ineluctable modality of the visible: at least that if no more, thought through my eyes. Signatures of all things I am here to read, seaspawn and seawrack, the nearing tide, that rusty boot. Snotgreen, bluesilver, rust: a sign of the times. Coloured signs. Limits of the diaphane. But he adds: in bodies. Then he was aware of them bodies before of them coloured. How? By knocking his sconce against them, sure. He was a tall, heavy, bulky man, with a red face and a loud voice and a laugh that sounded like a machine gun. His name was Private Blithe, and he was afraid.

Stephen walked. The beach was empty. The sand was grey. The sea was grey. The sky was grey. Everything was grey, the color of the world before color was invented, the color of the void, the color of nothing.

He walked and thought and the thoughts came like waves, one after another, each one different, each one the same:

I am here. I am in England. I am going to France. I am going to jump. I am going to fight. I am going to kill. I am going to die. Or I am not going to die. And if I do not die, I will have killed. And if I have killed, I will have to live with the killing. And living with the killing is worse than dying from the killing. And yet I do not want to die. And yet I do not want to kill. And yet I am here, on this beach, in this grey, in this war, and there is no way out except through.

He stopped. He picked up a stone. He threw it into the sea. The stone made a sound: plop. The sound was small and final and it was swallowed by the sea, which did not care about stones or men or wars or anything at all.

Plop.

The sound of a life ending.

The sound of a life beginning.

The sound of nothing.

He walked on.

## CALYPSO

### V.

Gerty MacDowell was a girl. She was a girl who lived in a house by the sea and she had a face like a flower and a heart like a stone and she was in love with a boy who did not know she existed, which was the way of love, which was the way of the world, which was the way of everything.

She sat on the beach and watched the soldiers. They were everywhere, these soldiers, like ants at a picnic, like locusts in a field, like the end of the world. They were young and old and tall and short and fat and thin and they all had the same look in their eyes: the look of men who had seen something they could not unsee and done something they could not undo and were waiting to see what would happen next.

She watched one soldier in particular. He was slim and pale and he walked alone and he looked at the sea as if the sea had answers to questions he had not yet asked. She did not know his name. She did not know his story. She knew only that he was beautiful in the way that broken things are beautiful, and she wanted to fix him, even though she knew that broken things cannot be fixed, only endured.

She walked toward him.

— Hello, she said.

He looked at her. His eyes were grey like the sea and the sky and the sand and everything.

— Hello, he said.

— Are you a soldier?

— Yes.

— Are you going to France?

— Yes.

— Are you afraid?

— Yes.

She took his hand. His hand was cold. Her hand was warm. The warmth flowed from her to him like water flowing downhill, like light flowing from a candle, like love flowing from a heart that has too much love and nowhere to put it.

— Come back, she said.

— I will try.

— Promise.

— I promise to try to promise.

She smiled. He smiled. The smiles were small and sad and they were the most honest thing either of them had ever done.

He walked away. She watched him go. The sea was grey. The sky was grey. The sand was grey. And the soldier was a small grey figure walking into a grey future, and the future was a door, and the door was closed, and behind the door was France, and in France was the war, and in the war was death, and in the war was life, and in the war was everything and nothing and the space between.

---

## LOTUS EATERS

### VI.

The church was cool and dark and it smelled of incense and old wood and the prayers of the faithful, which had soaked into the stones over the centuries like water into bread.

Stephen knelt. He did not pray. He did not know how to pray. His mother had prayed, and her prayers had not saved her, and his father had not prayed, and his father was still alive, which seemed to suggest that prayer was either useless or counterproductive.

But he knelt anyway, because kneeling was what you did in churches, and churches were where you went when you were afraid, and he was afraid.

The priest came. The priest was old and fat and his face was a map of wrinkles, each wrinkle a year, each year a sin, each sin a story.

— My son, said the priest.

— Father, said Stephen.

— You are a soldier.

— Yes.

— You are going to war.

— Yes.

— Do you seek absolution?

— I seek understanding.

— Understanding is not my department. I deal in forgiveness.

— Then I seek forgiveness.

— For what?

— For what I am about to do.

The priest was silent. The silence was long and heavy and it filled the church like water filling a bowl.

— You are going to kill, said the priest.

— Yes.

— And you want me to tell you that it is all right.

— Yes.

— I cannot tell you that.

— Then what can you tell me?

— I can tell you that God loves you. I can tell you that your sins will be forgiven. I can tell you that the men you kill will go to heaven if they are Catholic and somewhere else if they are not, and that the somewhere else is not my problem.

— That is not comforting.

— Comfort is not my department either. I deal in salvation.

— What is the difference?

— Salvation is forever. Comfort is temporary.

Stephen stood. He left the church. The sun was bright and the air was warm and the world was beautiful in the way that beautiful things are beautiful: indifferently, without concern for the feelings of those who observe them.

He thought of the hampter. The small rodent on the pea pod. The hampter did not go to church. The hampter did not seek absolution. The hampter did not worry about salvation or comfort or the difference between them. The hampter simply acted. Simply did what needed to be done. Simply was.

Stephen envied the hampter more than ever.

## HADES

### VI.

The ambulance rolled through the French countryside. The countryside was green and gold and it looked like a painting, except that paintings did not smell of death and burning rubber and the particular sweetness of a body that has stopped being a person and become a thing.

In the ambulance was a dead man. His name had been Private Blithe. He had been nineteen. He had never been to bed with a girl. He had been afraid of everything, especially the dark, especially the silence, especially the noise. And now he was dead, and the dark and the silence and the noise could not hurt him anymore, which was the only consolation, and it was a poor one.

Stephen sat beside the body. He had not known Blithe well. Blithe was a private and Stephen was an officer and there was a distance between them that rank creates and death erases. But now, in the ambulance, they were equal. The dead man and the living man. The one who had stopped and the one who continued. The one who had no more choices and the one who still had choices, though the choices were narrowing with every mile, every minute, every heartbeat.

The ambulance hit a pothole. The body shifted. The blanket fell away from the face.

The face was peaceful. That was the worst part. The face was peaceful in a way that the living face had never been. The fear was gone. The worry was gone. The constant, gnawing anxiety that had been Blithe's companion since birth was gone, and in its place was nothing, and the nothing was peaceful, and the peacefulness was terrible.

Stephen covered the face. He looked out the window. The countryside rolled past. A farmhouse. A church. A field of cows. A burned-out tank. A dead horse. A living tree. A dead man. A living man. The world was a mixture of the living and the dead, and the mixture was life, and life was the mixture, and the mixture was unbearable.

He thought of the hampter. The hampter did not think about death. The hampter thought about pea pods and burrows and the forest and the things that needed doing. The hampter was alive in a way that humans could never be alive, because the hampter did not know about death, or if the hampter knew about death, the hampter did not care, and not caring was its own kind of wisdom.

Stephen wept.

---

## AEOLUS

### VII.

The headquarters was a tent. The tent was full of maps and radios and men who spoke in acronyms and believed that acronyms were a substitute for thought.

— SNAFU, said one.

— FUBAR, said another.

— TARFU, said a third.

Stephen did not know what these words meant. He knew only that they were words that men used when the reality was too terrible to describe directly, so they described it indirectly, with letters, as if letters could contain the horror, as if naming the thing could control the thing, as if language could save them.

Captain Winters stood at the map. He was a small man with a quiet voice and steady hands and eyes that had seen too much and forgotten nothing.

— We attack at dawn, he said.

Dawn. The word was a knife. Dawn. The beginning of the day. The end of the night. The time when the world wakes up and remembers that it is still here, still alive, still capable of suffering.

— Where? asked Stephen.

— Here. Winters pointed to a spot on the map. A village. A name. A place where men would die.

— Why?

— Because it is there.

— That is not a reason.

— It is the only reason. The village is there. The enemy is there. We are here. Therefore we go there. Therefore we fight. Therefore we die. Therefore we live. Therefore we are here, in this tent, on this day, in this war, and there is no why, only where, and the where is a place on a map, and the map is a piece of paper, and the paper is a lie, because the map shows roads and rivers and hills but it does not show fear or courage or the sound a man makes when he is shot, and that sound is the only truth, and the truth is a sound, and the sound is silence.

Stephen said nothing. He looked at the map. The map was green and brown and blue and it was beautiful in the way that lies are beautiful: clean and simple and completely false.

He thought of the hampter. The hampter's map was a scrap of birch bark with berry juice marks. The hampter's map showed seven ward stones and the paths between them. The hampter's map was true because the hampter had made it himself, and you cannot lie to yourself, or if you can, the lie is a kind of truth, the truth of what you want to believe, and wanting to believe is its own kind of courage.

---

## SCYLLA AND CHARYBDIS

### VIII.

The choice was simple: die or die.

On the left, the Germans had a machine gun nest in a farmhouse. On the right, the Germans had an artillery battery in a wood. In the middle, Stephen and his men were crossing a field, and the field was open, and the field was flat, and the field was a killing ground.

— Left or right? asked the sergeant.

— Left is closer, said Stephen.

— Right is safer, said the sergeant.

— Nothing is safe.

— Then left.

They went left. The machine gun opened up. The sound was like the end of the world, which it was, for some of them. Men fell. Men screamed. Men died. And Stephen ran, and the running was everything, and the thinking was nothing, and the world was a tunnel of noise and fear and the desperate desire to reach the other side, the side where the machine gun was not, the side where life continued, the side where you could stop running and start shooting and maybe, maybe, maybe survive.

He reached the farmhouse. He threw a grenade. The grenade exploded. The machine gun stopped. The silence was louder than the noise.

He went inside. The room was dark. The room smelled of blood and gunpowder and the particular staleness of a place where men have died. There were three Germans. They were young. They were boys. They were someone's sons. They were the enemy.

Stephen shot them.

The first one died instantly. The second one died slowly. The third one did not die. The third one looked at Stephen with eyes that were blue and terrified and human, and Stephen looked at the third one with eyes that were grey and empty and not human at all, and the third one said something in German that Stephen did not understand but understood perfectly, and the something was "please" or "mother" or "why" or all of these at once, and Stephen shot him again, and the third one died.

Stephen walked out of the farmhouse. The sun was shining. The field was full of dead men. The dead men were American and German and they lay together in the grass, and the grass was green, and the blood was red, and the sky was blue, and the world was beautiful, and the beauty was obscene.

He thought of the hampter. The hampter had fought a Gloomworm. The Gloomworm was a creature of shadow and hunger and it had no face and no voice and no humanity, and killing it was easy, or at least easier than killing a boy with blue eyes who said "please" in a language you don't speak but understand.

Stephen envied the hampter.

He envied the hampter more than he had ever envied anything.

He would envy the hampter for the rest of his life, however long or short that life might be.

## THE WANDERING ROCKS

### IX.

The company moved through France like a stone through water, leaving ripples of destruction and memory and the particular silence that follows violence.

They moved through villages. The villages were empty or full, and the full villages were full of people who were either grateful or resentful or both, because liberation is a complicated thing, and freedom is not the same as safety, and safety is not the same as peace.

They moved through fields. The fields were green and gold and they hid mines and the mines were hidden and the hidden things were the most dangerous things, because you could see a German soldier and shoot him, but you could not see a mine, and the mine did not care about your courage or your fear or your desire to live, the mine simply was, and then you stepped on it, and then you were not.

They moved through forests. The forests were dark and quiet and they hid snipers and the snipers were patient and the patient things were the most terrifying things, because a machine gun is honest, it tells you where it is, but a sniper is silent, and the silence is a promise, and the promise is a bullet, and the bullet is the end.

Stephen moved with them. He moved and thought and the thoughts came like the movement, one step after another, one thought after another, each one leading to the next, each one leading nowhere.

He thought of Dublin. He thought of his mother. He thought of the teacher who said the textbook was wrong. He thought of the priest who said salvation was forever. He thought of Gerty MacDowell who said come back. He thought of Blithe who did not come back. He thought of the German boy with blue eyes who said please.

He thought of the hampter.

The hampter was three inches tall. The hampter had fought a Gloomworm. The hampter had saved the forest. The hampter had done all this without thinking, without doubting, without the constant, gnawing awareness of his own mortality that made every step a question and every question an answer and the answer always the same: you are going to die, the only question is when.

The hampter did not know this. The hampter simply acted. Simply did what needed to be done. Simply was.

And in the simply being, the hampter was free.

And in the not being free, Stephen was a prisoner.

And the prison was his mind.

And the mind was a prison.

And the prison was the world.

And the world was a war.

And the war was everything.

And everything was nothing.

And nothing was unbearable.

---

## SIRENS

### X.

The piano was out of tune. The bar was too small. The wine was too sweet. The company was too loud. And Stephen was too alone, even though he was surrounded by men, because loneliness is not the absence of people, it is the absence of connection, and connection is a rare thing in a war, where men are interchangeable and death is random and the only thing that matters is surviving until tomorrow.

The piano player played. The song was a French song, a love song, a song about a girl who waited for her soldier to return, and the soldier did not return, and the girl waited anyway, because waiting was all she had, and having nothing is still having something, and something is better than nothing, and nothing is unbearable.

The men sang. They sang in English and French and German and Italian and the languages mixed and merged and became a single sound, the sound of men trying to be human in a place that was trying to make them inhuman, and the trying was noble, and the nobility was futile, and the futility was beautiful.

Stephen drank. The wine was sweet and it made his head fuzzy and the fuzziness was a relief, because thinking was painful and not thinking was a kind of peace, and peace was a kind of death, and death was a kind of peace, and the circle was complete, and the completion was nothing, and nothing was—

— Dedalus!

The voice was Buck's. Buck was drunk. Buck was always drunk. Buck was drunk the way other men were sober: consistently, reliably, as a matter of principle.

— Dedalus, sing us a song!

— I don't sing.

— Everyone sings. Singing is what separates us from the animals.

— The animals don't kill each other.

— The animals kill each other all the time. They just don't write songs about it.

Stephen stood. He swayed. He sang. The song was an Irish song, a rebel song, a song about a man who fought the English and lost and was hanged and became a martyr and the martyr became a song and the song became a memory and the memory became a reason to fight and the fighting became a loss and the loss became a song and the circle was complete.

The men cheered. The men wept. The men drank. The men were alive, and being alive was a miracle, and miracles were rare, and rare things were precious, and precious things were temporary, and temporary things were unbearable.

Stephen sat down. He thought of the hampter. The hampter did not sing. The hampter did not drink. The hampter did not weep or cheer or do any of the things that humans did to remind themselves that they were human. The hampter simply was.

And in the simply being, the hampter was more human than any of them.

---

## CYCLOPS

### XI.

The giant was a German. The German was a giant. The giant had a gun. The gun was a machine gun. The machine gun was in a nest. The nest was in a building. The building was in a town. The town was in France. The France was in Europe. The Europe was in the world. The world was at war. The war was everything. And everything was the giant.

Stephen looked at the giant. The giant looked at Stephen. They were fifty yards apart. The distance was nothing. The distance was everything. The distance was the difference between life and death, and the difference was a bullet, and the bullet was a piece of metal, and the metal was a piece of the earth, and the earth was a piece of the universe, and the universe was a piece of nothing, and nothing was—

The giant fired.

The bullets came. The bullets were a stream of metal, a river of death, a waterfall of lead. Stephen dove. The bullets passed over him. The bullets hit the wall behind him. The wall crumbled. The crumbling was the sound of civilization ending, which it was, which it always was, which it always would be.

Stephen returned fire. The rifle kicked. The kick was a punch. The punch was a reminder. The reminder was that he was alive, and being alive was painful, and pain was proof, and proof was all he had.

The giant fell.

The giant was a man. The man was a boy. The boy was someone's son. The son was dead. The dead was nothing. The nothing was—

Stephen moved on.

---

## NAUSICAA

### XII.

The girl was on the beach. The beach was in Normandy. The Normandy was in France. The France was in Europe. The Europe was in the world. The world was at war. And the girl was on the beach, and the beach was beautiful, and the beauty was obscene.

She was collecting shells. The shells were white and pink and they were perfect in the way that shells are perfect: small and complete and empty. She held them to her ear and listened and the listening was a kind of prayer, and the prayer was a kind of hope, and the hope was a kind of madness, because there was no hope, not here, not now, not in this world of blood and sand and the constant, grinding machinery of war.

Stephen watched her. He watched from a distance. The distance was safe. The distance was lonely. The loneliness was familiar. The loneliness was home.

She looked up. She saw him. She smiled.

The smile was a crack in the world. A crack that let the light in. A crack that let the darkness out. A crack that was both and neither and everything in between.

She waved.

He waved back.

The waving was a conversation. The conversation was a connection. The connection was a moment. The moment was a memory. The memory was a treasure. The treasure was temporary. The temporary was unbearable.

He walked away. He did not look back. Looking back was dangerous. Looking back was remembering. Remembering was feeling. Feeling was breaking. Breaking was dying. And he was not ready to die.

Not yet.

Not today.

Maybe tomorrow.

But not today.

---

## OXEEN OF THE SKY

### XIII.

The sky was on fire.

The sky was always on fire. The sky had been on fire for four years. The fire was bombs and bullets and the burning of cities and the burning of men and the burning of everything that had ever been good or beautiful or worth saving.

Stephen lay in a foxhole. The foxhole was a grave. The grave was a home. The home was a hole in the ground. The ground was France. The France was Europe. The Europe was the world. The world was burning.

He thought of the hampter.

The hampter had faced a storm. The storm was green. The storm was magic. The storm was the Zunda Storm, and the Zunda Storm was a test, and the test was a choice, and the choice was to care or not to care, and caring was the only thing that mattered, and the hampter had cared, and the caring had saved the forest.

Stephen cared. He cared about the men beside him. He cared about the men across from him. He cared about the girl on the beach. He cared about the German boy with blue eyes. He cared about the world that was burning and the world that would come after and the world that might never come at all.

And the caring was not enough.

The caring was never enough.

The caring was all there was.

The bombs fell. The bombs were a rain. The rain was metal. The metal was death. The death was—

Yes.

The word came from somewhere. The somewhere was deep. The somewhere was inside. The somewhere was the place where the hampter lived, the small rodent on the pea pod, looking at the burning horizon, and the looking was a choice, and the choice was to act, and the action was to care, and the caring was to say yes, and the yes was—

Yes.

I said yes.

I will act.

I will care.

I will say yes.

The bombs fell.

Stephen said yes.

## CIRCE

### XIV.

The dream was a city. The city was Dublin. The city was London. The city was Paris. The city was Berlin. The city was every city and no city. The city was the city of the mind, and the mind was a city, and the city was a war, and the war was a dream, and the dream was—

Stephen walked. The streets were full of people. The people were soldiers. The people were civilians. The people were dead. The people were alive. The people were both. The people were neither.

He saw his mother. His mother was young. His mother was old. His mother was dying. His mother was dead. His mother was alive. His mother was a memory. The memory was a ghost. The ghost was a feeling. The feeling was guilt. The guilt was a weight. The weight was a stone. The stone was a grave. The grave was a door. The door was—

He saw the hampter. The hampter was three inches tall. The hampter was three feet tall. The hampter was three meters tall. The hampter was the size of the world. The hampter was the size of nothing. The hampter was looking at him. The looking was a judgment. The judgment was a mercy. The mercy was a forgiveness. The forgiveness was—

— You are not the hampter, said the hampter.

— I know, said Stephen.

— The hampter is small. You are large. The hampter is simple. You are complex. The hampter acts. You think. The hampter is free. You are a prisoner.

— I know, said Stephen.

— Then why do you not act?

— Because I think.

— Then stop thinking.

— I cannot.

— Then you will die.

— I know.

The hampter smiled. The smile was small and sad and it was the most honest thing Stephen had ever seen.

— The hampter dies too, said the hampter. The hampter dies and the forest dies and the world dies and everything dies. But the hampter says yes. The hampter acts. The hampter cares. And the caring is enough. The caring is always enough. The caring is the only thing that is enough.

— I am not the hampter.

— No. But you can say yes.

Stephen woke. The foxhole was dark. The bombs had stopped. The silence was a sound. The sound was a presence. The presence was—

Yes.

## EUMAEUS

### XV.

The port was Southampton. The Southampton was in England. The England was in Europe. The Europe was recovering. The recovery was slow. The slow was painful. The painful was necessary. The necessary was—

Stephen sat in a pub. The pub was called The Horse and the Horse was dead and the dead was a memory and the memory was a drink and the drink was a forgetting and the forgetting was a mercy.

The man beside him was old. The old man was a sailor. The sailor had been to sea. The sea was a mystery. The mystery was a story. The story was a lie. The lie was a truth. The truth was—

— You were in the war, said the old man.

— Yes, said Stephen.

— I was in the last war. The Great War. The war to end all wars. The war that didn't end all wars. The war that ended nothing and began everything.

— I know.

— You think this war is different.

— Is it?

— No. Wars are the same. The uniforms change. The weapons change. The reasons change. But the war is the same. The war is men killing men for reasons they don't understand in places they've never been for people they've never met.

— Then why do we fight?

— Because the alternative is surrender. And surrender is death. And death is the end. And the end is nothing. And nothing is unbearable.

Stephen drank. The drink was bitter. The bitter was familiar. The familiar was home.

— I knew a man once, said the old man. A small man. A quiet man. A man who did his duty without complaining and died without ceremony and was buried without a marker. And I thought: that is the best kind of man. The kind who does what needs to be done without needing to be thanked or remembered or honored. The kind who simply acts. Simply cares. Simply is.

— A hampter, said Stephen.

— A what?

— Nothing. A story I heard once.

The old man nodded. The nod was understanding. The understanding was a connection. The connection was a moment. The moment was a memory. The memory was a treasure. The treasure was temporary. The temporary was—

Unbearable.

But bearable.

But bearable.

---

## ITHACA

### XVI.

The question was: what?

What was the war? What was the point? What was the purpose? What was the meaning? What was the answer? What was the question? What was—

Stephen walked. The walk was a journey. The journey was a return. The return was a homecoming. The homecoming was a recognition. The recognition was—

He was in Dublin. The Dublin was the same. The Dublin was different. The Dublin was the Dublin of his childhood and the Dublin of his memory and the Dublin of his imagination and the real Dublin, the Dublin that existed outside his head, the Dublin that did not care about his war or his guilt or his hampter.

He walked to his house. The house was empty. The empty was a presence. The presence was an absence. The absence was—

His mother was dead. His father was gone. His siblings were scattered. The house was a shell. The shell was a memory. The memory was a ghost. The ghost was—

He sat. The sitting was a rest. The rest was a peace. The peace was a silence. The silence was—

He thought of the hampter. The hampter was in the forest. The forest was safe. The safe was a ward. The ward was a stone. The stone was a crystal. The crystal was a light. The light was a green. The green was a life. The life was a—

Yes.

The word came from somewhere. The somewhere was deep. The somewhere was inside. The somewhere was the place where the hampter lived, the small rodent on the pea pod, looking at the horizon, and the horizon was not burning, the horizon was green, the green was life, the life was—

Yes.

Stephen said yes.

The yes was a choice. The choice was an action. The action was a life. The life was a—

Yes.

And the yes was enough.

And the yes was always enough.

And the yes was the only thing that was enough.

## PENÉLOPE

### XVII.

Yes because he never knew the hampter except as a story except as a dream except as a small round shape in the corner of his eye that disappeared when he looked directly at it yes because the hampter was three inches tall and he was six feet tall and the difference was everything and nothing yes because the hampter acted and he thought and the acting was better than the thinking or maybe the thinking was better than the acting or maybe they were the same thing seen from different angles yes because the hampter cared and he cared and the caring was the same caring and the same caring was enough and enough was all there was and all there was was enough yes because the hampter said yes and he said yes and the yes was the same yes and the yes was the only word and the only word was yes and yes was the beginning and the end and the beginning and the end and the—

Yes.
